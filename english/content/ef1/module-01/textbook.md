**Tom:** Thank you, I will. Nice talking with you, Anna.

**Anna:** Likewise, Tom. Enjoy the rest of the conference.

## Key Concepts

Low-Context Cultures

- Communication is direct and explicit.

- Information is clearly stated; little is left to interpretation.

- Focus is on task, efficiency, and clarity.

- Relationships are less crucial for conveying meaning.

### Example

Two professionals meet at a conference. They shake hands firmly, look each other in the eyes, and say: “Good day, I am Müller. Nice to meet you.”

High-Context Cultures

- Communication is indirect and implicit.

- Meaning relies on context, relationships, and shared knowledge.

- Focus is on harmony, respect, and social cues.

- Relationships and trust are often more important than words alone.

### Example

An employee meets his new co-worker. He bowed slightly while saying, “Nice to meet you. I am Tanaka.” The other returned a slightly deeper bow before introducing himself.

## Vocabulary: Describing a Company’s Structure

When introducing a company’s structure, you can use these phrases:

- To explain the general organization of a company: o Our company is structured into multiple departments.

- To describe who your direct manager is: o I report to [position].

- To explain a team’s role in the company: o This team is responsible for [task].

**Example:** Our company is structured into multiple departments. I work in the Software Development Department, and I report to the Team Lead. This team is responsible for backend development and system optimization.

## Key Phrases: How to confirm information

Confirming information is an important communication skill in professional settings. It helps you check that you have understood something correctly and avoid misunderstandings.

| Confirming | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Tag questions –<br>“right?” | • He has a meeting this morning, right?<br>• We’ve already sent the proposal, right? |
| 2. Tag questions –<br>auxiliary verb | • He went to the trade fair in San Francisco, didn’t he?<br>• I'm going to get an email with the details, aren't I?<br>• He hasn’t finished the presentation, has he? |
| 3. Negative<br>questions | • Doesn’t she work in International Sales?<br>• Isn’t he responsible for the project?<br>• Didn’t we meet at the conference last year? |

<!-- Source: F1_M01_Textbook.pdf, page 22 -->

## Grammar: Tag Question

### 1. Definition

- A short question added to the end of a statement to turn it into a question.

- Used to confirm information or seek agreement.

### 2. Rules

| Rules | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Positive Statement, Negative Tag | You are coming, aren't you? |
| 2. Negative Statement, Positive Tag | He hasn’t finished the presentation, has<br>he? |
| 3. Auxiliary verb / Modal verb | • You can stay for the call, can’t you?<br>• We should send the invoice today,<br>shouldn’t we?<br>• They will confirm the booking, won’t<br>they? |
| 4. Special cases: |   |
| I am | I'm going to get an email with the details,<br>aren't I? |
| Must: Obligation or Necessity (Present<br>or Future) → use modal | You must finish your work, mustn't you? |
| Must: Assumption or Deduction<br>→ use verb | • She must be tired, isn't she?<br>• He must have left, hasn't he? |
| Indefinite pronoun (people)<br>→ Pronoun in the tag = They | • Someone called, didn't they?<br>• Nobody was here, were they? |
| Indefinite pronoun (thing)<br>→ Pronoun in the tag = It | Everything is still alright, isn’t it? |
| This/That<br>→ Pronoun in the tag = It | This is your book, isn't it? |
| These/Those<br>→ Pronoun in the tag = They | These are your shoes, aren't they? |
| “I think”, “I believe”, “I suppose”<br>→ The tag question is formed based<br>on the main clause | I think she is coming, isn't she? |

Catalyst Creative Solutions, an IT company based in the United States, was contracted by Kaito Electronics, a Japanese company, to develop a new cloud-based data management system. ## Pronunciation: Linking sounds

1. Definition: Linking sounds is when the end sound of one word connects to the beginning sound of the next word in a sentence, creating a smoother, more natural flow, often making words sound like one.

2. Common Pattern: One of the most frequent ways we link sounds in English is consonant - vowel. This happens when a consonant at the end of a word linked to a vowel sound at the start of the next word.

## Vocabulary: Direct vs. Indirect Requests

1. Softening Language – used to reduce directness of a message, making it less confrontational or aggressive.

## Problem Solving Strategy

How to Identify Problems and Propose Solutions

1. Define the situation - State facts clearly and objectively.

## Production

### A - DEBATE

**Topic:** “Is it better to address workplace problems privately or in a team meeting?”

1. Students go into 2 teams - assign Side A (private) or Side B (in a team meeting).

2. Internal planning (3-4 minutes):

- Prepare 2-3 main arguments supporting your side.

- Think of 1-2 clarification questions to challenge the other team’s points.

3. Debate Round:

- Teams take turns presenting one argument at a time.

- After each argument, the opposing team asks one clarification question (e.g., “Could you explain what you mean by…?”).

- Repeat the process until each team has presented all arguments.

4. Each team uses resolution linkers to connect arguments to outcomes.

5. Class votes: which team gave the clearest, most convincing arguments?

Voting Criteria

A. Content & Relevance - How clear, relevant, and well-supported the arguments are. (5)

B. Language & Professionalism - Use of business-appropriate language, correct grammar, paraphrasing, and resolution linkers. (5)

C. Engagement & Interaction - Active listening, respectful responses, and addressing opposing points effectively. (5)

D. Organization & Clarity - Logical structure and clear presentation of ideas. (5)
